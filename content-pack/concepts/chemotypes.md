## What it is

<!-- framing -->
A chemotype is a chemically distinct form within a single species. The plants look alike and carry the same name, but they fill their oil glands with different molecules.

Thyme is the textbook case. The EMA assessment notes at least six chemotypes of *Thymus vulgaris*, and only the thymol type meets the European Pharmacopoeia's requirements for the herb [9]. Within one thymol-type cultivar, 'Standard Winter', thymol still ranged from 36.7 to 54.6 % of the oil between plants [20].

Basil varies between cultivars. In a GC-MS study of seven cultivars, linalool was found in all of them, estragole was a main compound only in 'Lettuce Leaf', and every cultivar except 'Lettuce Leaf' was rich in methyleugenol [18]. 'Cinnamon' basil carried 24.7 % methyl cinnamate, second only to linalool [19]. Because estragole and methyleugenol are the basil constituents the EMA treats as genotoxic carcinogens in rodents [4,5], which chemotype a basil belongs to is a safety question as well as a question of scent.

Rosemary has two recognised oil types. The pharmacopoeial ranges quoted by the EMA give a Spanish type with 18-26 % α-pinene and 16-25 % 1,8-cineole, and a Moroccan/Tunisian type with 38-55 % 1,8-cineole [12]. Greek oregano is usually carvacrol-rich, at 39.8 % of the oil in one cultivated sample [21], but three chemotypes were described from wild plants, one carvacrol and thymol type (23.3 % and 22.2 %), one dominated by thymol and one by linalyl acetate and linalool [22].

## The key idea in one picture

Use Figure 1. Put a finger on geranyl diphosphate and follow the arrows out of it: which terpene synthase a plant expresses decides which branch it takes. In basil, a line dominated by linalool synthase makes linalool, and a line carrying a frameshift in that gene makes geraniol instead [121]. On the phenylpropanoid side, two closely related O-methyltransferases turn chavicol into estragole and eugenol into methyleugenol, and a single amino-acid difference sets which one an enzyme prefers [120]. A chemotype is what the plant's particular set of enzymes produces.

## The maths, gently

<!-- framing -->
Nothing heavier than a range. A chemotype-dependent value is reported as a closed interval with a source for each end, and it is never collapsed to a single mean, because the mean of a thymol-type and a linalool-type plant describes no plant at all.

## How this paper uses it

Oil percentages in the catalogue are species-level ranges with their sources, and cultivar values are used only when the source names the cultivar. Even then they are the study's plant: the thyme data come from 'Standard Winter' [20], which is relevant to the greenhouse's 'German Winter' but not the same record. The oregano page gives 39.8 % and 23.3 % carvacrol from the papers read, rather than the higher figure often repeated [21,22]. No paper read names a linalool chemotype of thyme, so that occurrence row stays unverified even though the EMA confirms that several chemotypes exist [9].

<!-- synthesis -->
The practical consequence for the greenhouse is that none of its aromatic plants has a known chemotype until its own oil is analysed; the catalogue can say what the species has been found to make, and how widely that varies, but not what is on the bench [9,12,18,20,22].
