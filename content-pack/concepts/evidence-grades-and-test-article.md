## What it is

<!-- framing -->
Two questions sit behind every health statement on this site. How strong is the human evidence? And what, exactly, was tested? The first is answered by a letter; the second by a named test article. Neither makes sense without the other.

The letter runs from A to E, plus T. A is a systematic review or meta-analysis of human randomised controlled trials; B is at least one human randomised trial; C is observational or small uncontrolled human work; D is animal studies; E is laboratory or computer work; T is traditional use recorded in an official monograph, such as the EMA's listing of thyme for productive cough associated with a cold [9]. A D or E finding is never phrased as an effect in people, and a T grade is phrased as "traditionally used for", never as "helps".

The test article is the material a study actually used: the whole herb, a named extract, an essential oil, an isolated compound or a multi-herb formula. It matters because the same plant name covers very different chemical mixtures. The strongest human evidence attached to peppermint comes from a meta-analysis of 12 randomised trials in 835 patients of peppermint oil in enteric-coated capsules for irritable bowel symptoms [54]. The leaf's dominant phenolic, by contrast, is the flavanone glycoside eriocitrin, and a hot-water infusion carries about three-quarters of the leaf's polyphenols but only 20-25 % of its oil [6]. A tea made in one analysis contained 14 terpenes against the 70 compounds of peppermint oil [129]. So the grade A belongs to the capsule. It is not evidence about the tea, and it is not evidence about menthol alone, even though menthol is the oil's cooling molecule and acts on the cold receptor TRPM8 [38].

## The maths, gently

<!-- framing -->
There is no formula here, only a rule of attachment. Write a graded statement as a triple: (claim, grade, test article). The grade is a property of the whole triple. Changing the test article produces a new triple whose grade has to be established from its own evidence, and it may have none.

## How this paper uses it

The sage page shows why the rule is needed. The EMA assessed the sage trials for hot flushes and cognition and declined to accept well-established use, because the products tested did not correspond to products on the EU market [1]; a systematic review of the cognition trials found benefits undermined by methodological problems and by the mix of extracts, oils and raw herb used [53]. Each sage trial is therefore graded for its own preparation. The same applies to the chamomile anxiety trials, which tested an extract in capsules [57], to the thyme cough trial, whose test article was a thyme and ivy combination syrup [56], and to the baicalin study, in which isolated baicalin reduced rosuvastatin exposure in healthy volunteers [33]: that is a grade B finding for baicalin, not for skullcap tea.

<!-- synthesis -->
Read together, these cases show that the letter alone would mislead in nearly every profile on this site; it is the pairing with a test article that stops strong evidence for one preparation spreading to its relatives [1,6,33,54,129].
