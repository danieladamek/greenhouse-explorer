## 1. What every plant shares

<!-- framing -->
The plants on the greenhouse benches look like a catalogue of differences. This chapter starts with what they share, because the differences only make sense against it.

Every one of them builds its cells inside walls of cellulose [101,102], captures light with chlorophyll [105,107], and feeds the carbon it fixes into a small set of central pathways [106,110,116]. From those central pathways branch the side roads that make each species chemically distinct: the routes to menthol in peppermint, thymol in thyme and the rosmarinic acid that almost every herb here shares [42,113,118]. One historical review counts more than 200,000 such secondary metabolites [108].

<!-- framing -->
The rest of this primer follows the carbon from the wall, through the leaf, along three pathways and finally into a cup of hot water.

<!-- section: 2-the-cell-wall-and-cellulose -->
## 2. The cell wall and cellulose

A plant cell sits inside a wall. In a growing cell that wall is made of cellulose microfibrils embedded in a hydrated matrix of hemicelluloses and pectins [102]. Cellulose itself is a linear polymer of glucose [103], and it is the most abundant biopolymer on Earth [104]. The wall is what lets a plant stand without a skeleton: turgor pressure pushes each cell outward, the cellulose scaffold resists, and the plant's whole growth habit rests on that balance [101].

The microfibrils are spun at the cell surface. Protein complexes in the plasma membrane, about 30 nm across and containing roughly 36 cellulose synthase (CESA) subunits, make the glucan chains as they travel through the membrane [101]. Their tracks follow the microtubules lying just beneath it, which is how a cell steers the direction in which its wall is reinforced [101]. To grow, the cell loosens the wall in a controlled way: proteins called expansins disrupt non-covalent bonds between wall polymers and let it relax under turgor [102].

<!-- synthesis -->
For the tea pages the wall matters practically: the scaffold stays in the strainer, and what the cup receives is set by what dissolves out of it, not by what the leaf contains [6,102,131].

<!-- section: 3-photosynthesis-and-chlorophyll -->
## 3. Photosynthesis and chlorophyll

The green of every leaf in the greenhouse is chlorophyll. Higher plants make four kinds of tetrapyrrole, chlorophyll, heme, siroheme and the phytochrome chromophore, from one shared pathway that begins with the amino acid glutamate [107]. Its intermediates are toxic if they pile up, and enzymes such as magnesium chelatase keep the flow at safe levels [107].

Chlorophyll works inside four large complexes in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast: photosystem II, the cytochrome b6f complex, photosystem I and ATP synthase [105]. Photosystem II uses a cluster of four manganese ions with calcium and chloride to split water and release oxygen. Electrons then pass along the chain to make NADPH, while the proton gradient the chain builds drives ATP synthesis [105].

The NADPH and ATP pay for carbon fixation. Rubisco catalyses the first step of net photosynthetic CO2 assimilation, but oxygen competes with CO2 at its active site and the enzyme is intrinsically slow, which is why it limits photosynthesis [106]. The plastid route to terpenoids starts from pyruvate and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate [110], and the shikimate pathway, which feeds the phenolics on this site, starts from phosphoenolpyruvate and erythrose 4-phosphate [116].

<!-- section: 4-primary-versus-secondary-metabolism -->
## 4. Primary versus secondary metabolism

Biochemists distinguish primary metabolism, which every cell needs to live, from secondary or specialised metabolism, which varies between lineages. For about 150 years phytochemistry concentrated on isolating and describing the second kind, and the compounds were long regarded as waste products [108].

That view changed as tracer studies, enzymology and then genetics mapped the routes; molecular evidence showed that genes of primary metabolism had been duplicated and recruited to defensive and ecological roles [108]. The shikimate pathway shows how blurred the boundary is. It makes the aromatic amino acids that go into every protein, it also supplies the precursors of pigments, hormones and cell-wall components, and in vascular plants at least 30 % of photosynthetically fixed carbon passes through it [116].

<!-- synthesis -->
The line between the two kinds is therefore better read as a set of branch points than as a wall. Phenylalanine is both a protein building block and the entry point to rosmarinic acid [116,118], and the C5 isoprenoid units that become menthol and thymol are the same units from which chloroplasts build carotenoids and the phytol tail of chlorophyll [110].

## 5. Three pathways that make the greenhouse smell

<!-- framing -->
Most of the molecules profiled on this site come from three routes. Figure 1 draws them on one page, and every end product in it links to its compound record.

<!-- figure: fig-pathways -->

The first is the terpenoid route. All isoprenoids are assembled from C5 prenyl diphosphate units, and plants make those units in two places: by the mevalonate (MVA) pathway in the cytosol and by the MEP pathway in plastids [111]. Both deliver isopentenyl diphosphate, from which larger isoprenoids are built by head-to-tail addition [110]. The division of labour is broad rather than absolute. The plastid route supplies monoterpenes (C10), diterpenes (C20), carotenoids and phytol, and the cytosolic route supplies sesquiterpenes (C15), sterols and triterpenes (C30) [110].

Monoterpenes begin when a terpene synthase folds geranyl diphosphate into a ring or a chain, and the choice of enzyme decides the smell. These synthases come in large gene families, and many make several products at once [112]. In sweet basil the linalool of the Genovese type comes from a linalool synthase, and a geraniol-rich cultivar turned out to carry a frameshift mutation in that gene [121]. Sage has separate synthases for sabinene, 1,8-cineole and bornyl diphosphate; sabinene leads on to the thujones and bornyl diphosphate to camphor [114]. Thyme cyclises geranyl diphosphate to γ-terpinene, which cytochrome P450 enzymes and a dehydrogenase then turn into thymol and carvacrol [42]. Peppermint needs eight enzymatic steps to reach (−)-menthol, starting with cyclisation to limonene and passing intermediates that include pulegone and menthone, all inside peltate glandular trichomes on the leaf surface [113].

Larger terpenoids follow the same logic with longer chains. Carnosic acid, the diterpene antioxidant of rosemary and sage, is proposed to be made from geranylgeranyl diphosphate via miltiradiene and ferruginol, and after harvest it oxidises to carnosol [43]. The widespread sesquiterpene β-caryophyllene binds the CB2 cannabinoid receptor [40]. Ursolic acid, a pentacyclic triterpene, comes from the cyclisation product α-amyrin through oxidation at carbon 28 by CYP716A enzymes, a step characterised in a legume and in grape rather than in the mints themselves [115].

The second route starts from phenylalanine. Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase removes its amino group to give cinnamic acid, the step that commits carbon to phenylpropanoid metabolism, and a cascade of oxygenases, reductases and transferases then builds lignin, coumarins, flavonoids and phenolic volatiles [117]. Rosmarinic acid is an ester of caffeic acid and 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid, made from phenylalanine and tyrosine together, and it is characteristic of the Boraginaceae and of the Nepetoideae, a subfamily of the mint family [118]. Basil uses the same route for its clove and anise notes. An NADPH-dependent reductase in its glandular trichomes makes eugenol from coniferyl acetate [119], and two closely related O-methyltransferases turn chavicol into estragole and eugenol into methyleugenol, their preference set by a single amino-acid difference [120]. Those two methylated products are the basil constituents the EMA treats as genotoxic carcinogens in rodents [4,5].

The third route branches from the second. Chalcone synthase condenses one p-coumaroyl-CoA with three malonyl-CoA units to make chalcone, the first product of the flavonoid pathway [123], so every flavonoid carries carbon from both phenylalanine and malonyl-CoA [122]. The flavones apigenin and luteolin belong here, and in chamomile, sage and thyme they occur largely as glycosides [10,20,132]. Skullcap shows how far a lineage can push one branch. *Scutellaria baicalensis* has evolved a root-specific pathway to 4′-deoxyflavones such as chrysin and wogonin [28], and one comparison measured baicalin at 26.1 mg/g in its root and 11.7 mg/g in *S. lateriflora* leaf [24].

<!-- section: 6-why-plants-make-these-molecules -->
## 6. Why plants make these molecules

In 1959 Gottfried Fraenkel argued that compounds with no nutritional role act as repellents or attractants, which would explain both their diversity and the narrow host ranges of plant-feeding insects [109]. The paper met years of near silence before it helped found chemical ecology as a discipline [109]. Its view has become the working one: secondary metabolites are now treated as part of a plant's survival strategy rather than as waste [108].

Defence is the best documented job. Glandular trichomes, found on roughly 30 % of vascular plants, produce, store and secrete large amounts of terpenoids, phenylpropenes and flavonoids; some of these are directly toxic to herbivores, and trichome density is among the main factors correlating with resistance to herbivory [126]. That is where the mints keep their oils. Menthol is made in peppermint's peltate glands [113], and the enzymes that make basil's estragole and methyleugenol sit mainly in the peltate trichomes of young leaves [120].

Scent is also a signal. More than 1,000 low-molecular-weight compounds are known to be emitted by plants, terpenoids first among them, from flowers, leaves and roots, and plants can raise their emissions sharply after herbivore damage, beyond the injured site itself [125]. Some act on animal receptors: menthol activates the cold receptor TRPM8 [38], and α-thujone blocks the GABA-A receptor's chloride channel, the basis of its convulsant toxicity [39].

Flavonoids work as pigments, as UV screens and as signals [122]. The screening role has been shown directly by genetics: Arabidopsis mutants lacking leaf flavonoids and related phenolics are highly sensitive to damage from UV-B light [124].

<!-- synthesis -->
These jobs help explain a pattern that recurs on the tea pages. The defensive oils are stored in surface glands and are small, volatile and poorly soluble in water, while the phenolic acids and flavonoid glycosides, UV screens among them, are the fraction hot water extracts well [6,113,124,126,130,131].

## 7. From leaf to cup

<!-- framing -->
Infusion, decoction and tincture are all extractions: a solvent, a temperature and a time applied to plant tissue. This section reads each of them as chemistry, using the greenhouse's own tea herbs, and Figure 2 collects the measured values it draws on.

<!-- figure: fig-extraction -->

Hot water extracts the phenolic fraction far better than the oil. The EMA assessment of peppermint leaf reports that an infusion takes up about 75 % of the leaf's polyphenolic compounds but only 20–25 % of its essential oil after 10 minutes with boiling water [6]. A study of lemon balm tea found the same asymmetry, with 93 % of the polyphenols and 31 % of the essential oil reaching the infusion [131]. For chamomile the EMA gives about 10–15 % of the oil constituents, depending on how the tea is prepared [10]. The oil molecules are poor solutes: menthol is almost insoluble in water [130], and the terpenes found in peppermint tea are a narrower set than the oil's, 14 compounds against 70 in one analysis [129].

That is the peppermint contrast. The strongest human evidence attached to peppermint concerns enteric-coated oil capsules for irritable bowel symptoms [54], while the leaf's own dominant phenolic is the flavanone glycoside eriocitrin [6], and the infusion of a single commercial tea bag delivered up to 182 mg of water-soluble polyphenols in one analysis [128].

Time and temperature set the rate. For chamomile, extraction of phenolics followed pseudo-first-order kinetics and ran faster as water temperature rose from 57 to 100 °C [133]. In sage tea, thujone and camphor rose for about five minutes and then levelled off; commercial sage teas averaged 4.4 mg/L thujone for food teas and 11.3 mg/L for medicinal teas, and a water infusion recovered on average 30 % of the thujone that a 60 % ethanol extract released [127]. The EMA derives exposure values of 3.5 to 6.6 mg thujone per day from rodent seizure data [2], which is the frame the sage page uses for those in-cup numbers.

Ethanol shifts the balance. For sage leaf extracted at 60 °C for 30 minutes, 30 % ethanol recovered 3,634 mg of rosmarinic acid per 100 g of dry leaf against 2,154 mg with water [132]. For thyme the EMA reports total phenolics best extracted with 60 % ethanol, at about 50 mg/g of dry herb, with less from 30 % ethanol and least from 96 % ethanol [9]. Carnosic acid, less polar again, was recovered best in hexane, reaching 13 % of a dried rosemary extract, while ethanol favoured rosmarinic acid [136]. Commercial lemon balm tinctures made from dried herb held 2.96–22.18 mg/mL rosmarinic acid, against no more than 0.92 mg/mL in tinctures from fresh herb, across products that also differed in herb-to-extract ratio and alcohol strength [135].

<!-- synthesis -->
Read together, these measurements describe a gradient rather than a rule. Water favours phenolic acids and glycosides, mixtures of water and ethanol reach the widest range, the least polar terpenoids need more ethanol or a non-polar solvent, and volatility then removes part of whatever oil the solvent does reach [6,9,127,132,136].

Heat can also make molecules that the plant never did. Chamomile flowers contain matricin, a proazulene, and during steam distillation it is at least partly converted into azulenes such as chamazulene; the pharmacopoeia describes the resulting oil as blue [10]. The same EMA report says matricin itself is extracted into teas at pharmaceutically relevant levels [10]. Whether any chamazulene forms in a cup of tea is not established by the sources read, so the chamomile page claims neither presence nor absence [10].

Drying is a chemical step too. Carnosol in rosemary and sage is an oxidative artefact of carnosic acid formed after harvest and during drying [43], and oven-drying chamomile at 80 °C lowered its phenolic content from 19.7 to 13 mg/g gallic-acid equivalents [133]. The greenhouse's one root drug, Chinese skullcap, has its baicalin reported per gram of dry root [24,27], but no study of what a simmered decoction extracts from it has yet been read for this site, so there is no decoction record for it.

## 8. How to read the rest of this site

<!-- framing -->
The plant, compound and tea pages follow a few conventions. They are this site's rules, not findings.

Every health statement carries a grade: A for a systematic review or meta-analysis of human randomised trials, B for at least one human randomised trial, C for observational or small uncontrolled human studies, D for animal studies, E for laboratory or computer studies, and T for traditional use recorded in an official monograph, as when the EMA lists thyme for productive cough associated with a cold [9].

Every grade also names its test article. Enteric-coated peppermint oil has grade A evidence for irritable bowel symptoms [54]; that grade belongs to the oil, not to menthol alone and not to peppermint tea, whose composition is dominated by other molecules [6,129]. The sage trials used named extracts and products that the EMA judged did not match products on the EU market [1], so the sage page grades each trial for its own preparation.

Amounts always carry their basis: per gram of dry leaf, per cent of essential oil and per litre of infusion are different quantities, never plotted together. Thymol makes up 36.7–54.6 % of the essential oil of one thyme cultivar [20], thujone in sage tea is reported in milligrams per litre of infusion [127], and baicalin in skullcap in milligrams per gram of dry tissue [24]. Essential-oil profiles vary with cultivar and chemotype, so they appear as ranges with their sources; estragole in basil runs from a minor constituent to a main one between cultivars [4,18].

Identity has a status too: confirmed, check or unresolved. The greenhouse's skullcap is unresolved, so the site profiles *S. lateriflora* and *S. baicalensis* side by side. The distinction matters because liver-injury reports attributed to skullcap have in some cases been traced to germander adulteration or mislabelling [13], and analytical methods can now tell the genera apart [25,26].
